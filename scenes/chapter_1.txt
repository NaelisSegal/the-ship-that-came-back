[b]Chapter 1. Lights in the windows[/b]

The flight takes three hours. At first the [i]Wayfarer[/i] is only a dot. Then it grows into a shape.

*set hours_left -3

You know this ship. You walked through it a hundred times while it was being built. It was long, white and smooth, like a needle with a fat end.

That is not what you see now.

The ship is the right length. But its skin is covered in patches. Some are metal. Some are a grey, ridged stuff, like the inside of a seashell. Whole new rooms have been built onto the outside. They cling to the ship like wasp nests on a wall.

And the windows are lit. Hundreds of them. Thousands.

When the [i]Wayfarer[/i] left, sixty crew were awake. Everyone else was in cold sleep. Sixty people do not need thousands of lights.

"Are you seeing this?" you ask.

The shuttle locks onto the ship with a heavy thump. The door hisses open.

The air hits you first. It smells of wet soil, smoke and cooking. Ship air should smell of nothing.

A young person waits in the doorway. They look about sixteen. They are thin, with short dark hair. Their coat is sewn from at least three old crew uniforms. You can still read half a name tag on one sleeve.

"I'm Pell," they say. "I was picked to meet you."

Behind Pell, the corridor is full of people. A hundred, maybe more. Old men. Women holding babies. Children on their parents' shoulders. Nobody speaks. They all look at you.

You look down. The corridor floor is worn. A shallow groove runs down the middle of the metal. It is smooth as a river stone.

Feet made that groove. A great many feet, walking the same path, for a long, long time.

You built this floor. Twelve years ago it was new.

"How long?" you ask. "How long have you been gone?"

Pell frowns, as if it is a strange question. "By the ship's count, this is year three hundred and eleven," they say.

Three hundred and eleven years.

For you, it was twelve.

These people are not the crew. The crew died long ago. These are their great-great-great-grandchildren, and further down than that. Somehow, more than three hundred years passed on the ship. Only twelve passed on Earth. You do not know how. You built the engine, and you do not know how.

"The council is meeting about you now," Pell says. "The Speaker leads the council. She will see you later today. Until then, I'm told to take you where you want to go."

The bridge is the room where the ship is steered. You remember it bright and busy. Now it is dim and very quiet.

Most of the screens are dead. The live ones are wrapped in tape and patched with wire. One old woman sits in the big chair at the front, knitting. She looks up at you, then goes back to her knitting. Pell says she keeps the log and does not like to talk.

The log is still running on the main screen. You know this software. You helped install it.

You scroll back to the first day.

[i]Day 1. The jump worked. We are somewhere. But this is not our star.[/i]

The captain wrote that. Her name is at the bottom of every early entry: Captain Mara Venn. You met her once, before the launch. She shook your hand and said your engine had better work.

You open the star map from that first day. It shows a dim red star, alone in a lot of dark. It is not on any chart you know.

Then you see what is around the star, and you sit down.

There is a ring. It circles the star, very far out. It is thin and perfectly round. It is far too even to be natural.

Someone built it. Someone built a ring around a whole star.

[i]Day 40. They came to look at us.[/i]

[i]Day 41. We cannot tell if they are alive the way we are alive. They do not answer. But they do not hurt us.[/i]

[i]Year 2. We are allowed to stay. The crew has started calling this place the Garden.[/i]

So the Garden is the whole place: the red star, the ring, and whoever built it.

That is the answer to the question that ruined your life. The [i]Wayfarer[/i] did not blow up. Your engine did not fail. It sent the ship somewhere else, somewhere with builders of its own.

The log goes on for three hundred years. You do not have time to read it all.

The cold sleep hall runs almost the whole length of the ship. It holds two thousand sleep pods, in long rows. Each pod is a glass box the size of a bed. Inside, a person can sleep for years without getting older.

You stood here on launch day. Every pod was closed, frosted and humming.

Now almost every lid is open.

Most of the pods are empty and dusty. Some have been turned into planters. Small plants grow out of them, with dark blue leaves. Some hold folded clothes, or old shoes, or photographs. People have left them here like gifts.

"Over the years, they woke them," Pell says. "A few at a time. The ship needed more hands. More families."

"How many are still asleep?"

Pell points to the far end of the hall.

There, behind a rope, one block of pods is still closed. Their lids are frosted. Their small lights still glow green.

You count. Two hundred and twelve.

Two hundred and twelve people who left Earth twelve years ago. They have slept through three centuries. Their parents, husbands, wives and children are still alive back home. Some of them have probably been on the news this morning, crying and hoping.

"We call them the Sleepers," Pell says. "Nobody wakes them. They are being kept for when we came home."

"You are home," you say.

Pell does not answer.

Off to one side, away from the rest, stands a single pod. It is bigger and older-looking. Its lid is wide open, and the inside is still warm. Someone climbed out of it, and not long ago.

The name plate says: [i]Captain Mara Venn[/i].

The pod's screen shows its history. She went into this pod in year 290. Before that, there is no record of her sleeping at all. Not once, in two hundred and ninety years.

The lid was opened six days ago. That is the same day the ship came back.

Pell takes you down a ladder, then another, then through a heavy door.

You remember this place as the main cargo hold. It was a huge empty space, full of crates for the new world.

Now it is a town.

Floors have been built inside it, one above another, like a stack of shelves. Each floor is a field. Long lamps hang overhead, giving off a warm orange light. Under them grow rows of plants with dark blue leaves.

You walk past one plant, and its leaves turn to follow you. Slowly, like a sunflower following the sun.

"What are these?"

"Food," Pell says, surprised you need to ask. "They come from the Garden. That's where we lived, around another star."

People are everywhere. Children run along the paths between the fields. Old people sit in doorways, shelling seeds. There are homes built out of crates, and a school, and a market where people trade bowls of food for tools.

You try to guess how many people live here. You give up after a while. Many thousands. Far more than the ship was ever built to hold.

Then you notice the walls.

Someone has painted the same word, again and again, in big white letters: [i]home[/i].

Someone else has painted over many of them in red: [i]back[/i].

"The vote," Pell says, when they see you looking. "Last year the whole ship voted. Go home to Earth, or stay in the Garden forever. Home won."

"By how much?"

Pell is quiet for a moment. "Not much."

A man is standing at the edge of the nearest field. He has been watching you since you came in. He is maybe fifty, with soil on his hands. He walks over.

"Look at the leaves," he says to you. "Go on. Look."

You look. Up close, the blue leaves have brown edges. Some are curling.

"They're dying," he says. "They have been dying since the jump. They need the light of the Garden's star. These lamps are not enough. We will run out of food in a few months."

He leans in closer.

"You should have left us where we were," he says. Then he walks away.
